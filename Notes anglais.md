## **1. Friendship Nature & Expectations**

**Question:**  
How would you describe the nature of friendships in Finland compared to France?

**Answer:**  
I found Finnish friendships much slower to form. People were polite and friendly, but it took time before they invited me to their home. Once the friendship became “official,” it felt very loyal and stable. In France, it's easier to connect quickly, but relationships remain more flexible and less intimate at first.

**Short analysis (Hall – High/Low Context):**  
Finland’s communication style is **lower-context** than France: people rely less on implicit cues and more on direct, clear communication. This explains why Finns take longer before considering someone a true friend—relationships depend on explicit trust, not contextual warmth.

---

## **2. Notion of Time**

**Question:**  
What differences did you notice regarding punctuality and time management?

**Answer:**  
Finns are extremely punctual. Meetings start exactly on time, and arriving even two minutes late felt disrespectful. In France, being a few minutes late is usually tolerated socially.

**Short analysis (Hofstede – Long-Term Orientation):**  
Finland scores relatively high on long-term orientation and uncertainty avoidance, which promotes structure, planning, and punctuality. France, with a more flexible perception of time, is more tolerant of minor deviations.

---

## **3. Cleanliness & Personal Space**

**Question:**  
What was your experience with personal space and cleanliness standards?

**Answer:**  
Finns give each other a lot of space—queues, public transport, even during conversations. Cleanliness is also very important; people remove shoes at home and keep shared spaces tidy. As a French person, I noticed we stand a bit closer when talking and we’re more flexible about tidiness.

**Short analysis (Trompenaars – Specific vs. Diffuse):**  
Finland leans more **specific**—clear boundaries between personal and public space. France tends to be more **diffuse**, with more overlap in personal interactions.

---

## **4. Gender Roles**

**Question:**  
How did you see gender roles expressed in daily life?

**Answer:**  
There was a strong sense of equality. Men take parental leave, both partners cook, and it's normal to see women in leadership roles. It felt more balanced than what I'm used to in France.

**Short analysis (Hofstede – Masculinity vs. Femininity):**  
Finland is a **highly feminine society**, valuing equality and cooperation. France is more masculine, emphasizing competition and traditional gender role distinctions.

---

## **5. Importance of Silence**

**Question:**  
How did you adapt to Finnish attitudes towards silence in conversations?

**Answer:**  
At first, the silence felt awkward. But over time, I realized silence isn’t negative—people think before speaking. In France, silence often feels like a communication breakdown.

**Short analysis (Hall – High/Low Context):**  
Finland’s low-context communication sees silence as reflective and respectful, not ambiguous. French higher-context communication fills silence to maintain relational flow.

---

## **6. Leadership Style**

**Question:**  
Did you observe differences in leadership or hierarchy at work?

**Answer:**  
Finnish managers are very approachable and informal. Decisions are made collaboratively, and everyone’s opinion is valued. Coming from France, where hierarchy is more visible, it felt surprisingly horizontal.

**Short analysis (Meyer – Leading scale):**  
Finland is **egalitarian** in leadership (flat hierarchy), whereas France is more **hierarchical**. This affects decision-making and manager–employee interactions.

---

## **7. Concept of Fairness & Rules**

**Question:**  
How would you describe the Finnish approach to rules and fairness?

**Answer:**  
Finns follow rules strictly—traffic rules, queuing, paperwork. But it’s not blind obedience; it’s about fairness. Everyone respects the system because it protects everyone equally.

**Short analysis (Trompenaars – Universalism vs. Particularism):**  
Finland leans strongly **universalist** (rules apply equally). France is more **particularist**, where context can shape how rules are interpreted.

---

## **8. Attitude Toward Age**

**Question:**  
How did people in Finland treat age differences in social or professional settings?

**Answer:**  
Age didn’t seem to confer as much authority. Younger colleagues could disagree with older ones without tension. Respect is shown through competence rather than age.

**Short analysis (Hofstede – Power Distance):**  
Finland’s **low power distance** means age does not automatically equal authority. France’s higher power distance strengthens hierarchical respect for elders.

---

## **9. Raising Children & Education**

**Question:**  
What differences did you notice in how children are raised or educated?

**Answer:**  
Finnish kids are encouraged to be independent early—walking to school alone, making choices, solving problems. In France, there’s more supervision and structure.

**Short analysis (Meyer – Evaluating & Deciding):**  
Finland’s culture teaches **trust and self-direction**, reflecting a consensual decision culture. French education emphasizes evaluation and authority-based direction.

---

## **10. Saying Goodbye & Social Rituals**

**Question:**  
Did you find differences in the way people say goodbye or end social interactions?

**Answer:**  
Yes! Goodbyes in Finland are short and efficient—a quick “moi” or “hei hei.” In France, it’s often longer, with repeated goodbyes, sometimes kisses, and a gradual exit.

**Short analysis (Hall – High/Low Context):**  
French high-context culture uses extended goodbyes to reinforce social bonds. Finland’s low-context approach values clarity over ritual, making farewells concise.
